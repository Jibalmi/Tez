Research

How Tez reads a decision out of a frozen model, measured end to end: the mechanism, a reproduction of SemIf's benchmark, a voice-to-action loop, a head-to-head with Laya on its own datasets, and a probe lab that reads decisions from the middle of the network. The dead ends are in it too.

The report

A "System One" model answers a typed question (which option, yes or no, what level) by reading a probability distribution straight out of one forward pass, decoding nothing. The report shows that this works locally today with frozen open weights, measures where it breaks, and builds the use case it suits best. It was audited before release; its section 3.7 lists what the first draft got wrong and how each finding was corrected.

What it covers:

  • Mechanism and literature. How a typed decision is read from one forward pass, what Jev documents and what can only be inferred, why small models fail, and what "calibrated" has to mean.
  • SemIf's benchmark, reproduced. A frozen Gemma 4 12B reaches 0.943 mean-family balanced accuracy on 144 three-option decisions; SemIf's own 4B reproduces exactly at 0.813, and averaging six option orderings takes it to 0.912, statistically level with the 12B.
  • Latency as a systems problem. Constant material first, variable last, and --swa-full took a streamed voice decision from 205 ms to 30 ms per word.
  • Voice to action. 220 spoken commands, an early-commit policy with action classes, and one harmful action in 198 utterances.
  • Head-to-head with Laya on byte-identical rows and the same GPU, on Laya's datasets and in eleven languages, with the places Laya wins.
  • The probe lab. Which layer to read, how many labels a probe needs, where to cut the served model, a conformal error bound, and the ideas that did not work.
  • What a credible open decision model would have to show, the architecture that follows, the licences, and how to reproduce every run.

Findings worth knowing

From the project README, for anyone about to build something like this.

  • The frozen model knows more than its letter logits say. A per-question linear probe on the frozen Qwen3.5-4B's hidden state (12 layers below the top) scores 0.794 on typed-decisions — above Laya's fine-tuned 0.766 and Jev's 0.727 — from a model whose own one-pass letter readout gets 0.49. Minutes of logistic regression, no LLM training, and the top third of the network can be skipped.
  • Reading early is cheap latency. Truncating the 4B to 20–24 of its 32 layers cuts prefill 1.30–1.54× with no loss in probe accuracy. The same probe recipe on Laya's tasks beats Laya's fine-tuned checkpoint on five of six, and reaches 0.860 on Banking77 (Jev 0.870) in one pass.
  • Two cheap levers barely moved. A MiniLM shortlist lifts Banking77 from 0.713 to 0.738 in 1.1 passes; eliminate-then-rescore (PoE) is within ±2 points everywhere and is recorded as negative.
  • Worked examples in the cached prefix are free accuracy (+2 pts on 2,000 decisions, +5 on schema-specific questions); thinking budgets before the readout, ordinal expected-value readouts and digit/lowercase answer symbols are null or negative at 12B.
  • Symbol-logit decisions are scale-gated. A 3B model is at chance with 28 % order sensitivity and no calibration trick recovers it; a 4B sits at 0.646 with a third of decisions flipping under option reversal. Screen the backbone for symbol binding first.
  • Permutation averaging is the cheapest accuracy in the stack: +10 points on Qwen3.5-4B, +7 on Gemma 3 4B, in one batched forward pass.
  • Zhao et al. contextual calibration is harmful here (0.951 → 0.778, and averaging three placeholders does not rescue it): with the evidence blanked the model correctly answers "insufficient", so dividing that out deletes real answers. Never apply it to option sets that contain an abstain-style answer.
  • Prompt layout is a systems decision: constant material first, variable last. Transcript-last cut per-word latency 7× and raised accuracy (0.868 → 0.905).
  • A confident none on a partial transcript means "keep listening", never "do nothing" — the one rule that makes early commit work.

Source: README.md; the numbers behind each are in BENCHMARKS.md and on the benchmarks page.

The probe lab

A second round asked what makes the mid-depth probe usable on any schema, borrowing methods from other fields. Almost everything runs on one cache of every layer's hidden state of the frozen Qwen3.5-4B (and layers 0 to 40 of Gemma 4 12B) for the 8,000 typed-decisions prompts, so each idea is cheap to test and every table reuses the same states.

Each experiment as a chart of its own, titled with what it found; the numbers behind every one are in BENCHMARKS.md §4c. Select a chart to open it full size. The same charts on one page: tez_lab.png (2464 × 1771).

Headline results

  • The layer can be chosen without labels. The intrinsic dimension of unlabelled states bottoms out at layers 24 to 28 of the 4B, exactly the probe's accuracy plateau (0.79); the model's own confidence picks the wrong layer.
  • Cut the served model where the decision is made. The first 24 of 32 blocks keep a probe at 0.793 (full model 0.776) at 58 ms instead of 84, from a 3.53 GB file. The same cut lowers the zero-shot letters by 17.4 points on average across Laya's public suite: cut for probes, validate per task for letters.
  • Decide first, bind later. A probe trained on the usual option order still works on reversed options up to layer 14 and loses 20 points at layer 26: read at layer 18 if a schema may reorder its options.
  • A decision needs 64 numbers. One PCA learned from unlabelled traffic keeps 0.790 of 0.793, and a PCA learned on other workflows keeps 0.782 on a new one.
  • Label the typical rows first, and keep the zero-shot prior. Fifty typical labels per question on top of the 12B's zero-shot answers reach 0.766, the level of Laya's checkpoint fine-tuned on the whole train split.
  • Calibrated as it comes. A logistic probe has an ECE of 0.023 as read, against 0.262 for the 12B's raw letters at temperature 1 (0.051 at the default temperature Tez now applies) and 0.246 published for Jev.
  • A guaranteed error rate. Conformal selection acts on 40 % of decisions with at most 5 % wrong (realised 5.2 %).
  • Train in English, use in eleven languages. A MASSIVE probe trained on English rows only scores 0.803 macro over eleven languages (laya-multilingual 0.524).
  • The benchmark is now the limit. Every supervised route lands at 0.79 to 0.80. Where the benchmark's own label is decisive the probe is right 98 % of the time; where its labels are split, 56 %.

Source: BENCHMARKS.md §4c, experiments/probe_lab.py. Charts of the layer, label and conformal results are on the benchmarks page.

Negative results

What did not work, published and labelled as negative.

  • Thinking budgets before the readout: null or negative at 12B, at 2.0 to 4.3 s per decision.
  • A small-to-large cascade (Gemma 3 4B, then the 12B on disagreement): 0.854 at 117 ms, against the 12B alone at 0.951 at 110 ms.
  • Zhao et al. contextual calibration: accuracy 0.951 → 0.778, harmful whenever an option means "insufficient".
  • Eliminate-then-rescore: within ±2 points everywhere, at the cost of up to a second pass.
  • Probes trained on zero-shot answers only match their teacher (0.709 against 0.705); a Dawid–Skene label model over several readouts reaches 0.617.
  • Uncertainty-only labelling from a weak prior trains a worse probe than the same number of random labels.
  • Batch calibration of the teacher, DoLa, adaptive depth (it ties a fixed cut), LDA with an unlabelled covariance, and select-and-copy attention heads (0.50 to 0.53, the letters' level).
  • A question-agnostic letter probe does not transfer to a workflow it has never seen (0.521).
  • A collapsing commit bound for streaming voice: neither earlier nor as safe as the fixed threshold.
  • Small models cannot bind option letters: Llama 3.2 3B is at chance on SemIf's benchmark (0.358), with 28 % of decisions flipping when the options are reversed.

Sources: README.md, BENCHMARKS.md §4, §4b, §4c and §2.
